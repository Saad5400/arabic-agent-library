CHAPTER 1 — باب معرفة ما يضعه الناس في غير موضعه
Approx. pages: 8–37
Working value: lexical correction, semantic boundaries, anti-sloppiness in common usage.

Core editorial reading:
Ibn Qutaybah opens كتاب المعرفة by correcting words that people commonly place in the wrong slot. The governing habit is not mere dictionary recital; it is writerly discipline. He distinguishes near-words, names the specific field each belongs to, and warns against flattening them into casual synonymy.

Selected corrected distinctions and practical takeaways:

1) أشفار العين vs الهدب
- The source distinguishes the eyelid-edge from the lashes that grow on it.
- Practical use: do not call the lashes themselves "أشفار" without care; the writer should separate the border from the hair upon it.
- Writing value: precision in anatomical and descriptive prose.

2) إبرة العقرب/الزنبور vs sting/venom confusion
- The text distinguishes the actual instrument of harm from the poison carried with it.
- Writing value: when describing injury or danger, separate the implement, the effect, and the substance.

3) الطرب
- Ibn Qutaybah rejects the narrowed popular use that confines الطرب to joy.
- He treats it as agitation/lightness of the soul that may arise from joy or grief.
- Writing value: avoid reducing emotionally mixed words to one modernized mood.

4) الحشمة
- He resists collapsing الحشمة into simple shyness alone.
- In the opening examples it leans toward anger/strained emotional reaction more than mere bashfulness.
- Writing value: do not project later softened usage backward onto older prose without warning.

5) القافلة
- The source marks the returning caravan as the stricter sense, while later usage broadens.
- Writing value: a careful writer should know when a word’s classical edge is narrower than current habit.

6) المأتم / المناحة
- He separates the gathering of women in grief from the lamentation scene itself.
- Writing value: distinguish the social gathering, the act of lamenting, and the emotional state.

7) الحمام / اليمام
- The source gives a more exact bird distinction than common speech usually preserves.
- Writing value: useful for literary description and for avoiding flattened zoological vocabulary.

8) الربيع / الفصول
- Ibn Qutaybah reports that Arabs differed in season naming and that common people use spring labels loosely.
- Writing value: season words may require contextual caution; some are convention-bound, not self-evident.

9) الظل / الفيء
- The chapter sharply distinguishes general shade from the shade that returns/leans after the sun has shifted.
- Writing value: one of the most useful distinctions for elevated prose, descriptive writing, and Qur'anic or classical allusion.

10) الكلأ / السراب and other near-field terms
- The chapter repeatedly warns that surface resemblance tempts misuse.
- Writing value: semantic neighborhoods require border control.

11) names of body parts, animals, weapons, weather, food, garments, and earth-materials
- The chapter keeps expanding from one-off corrections to a whole habit of lexical sorting.
- Writing value: precision is not restricted to abstract eloquence; it extends to daily nouns and technical reference.

Editorial summary of the chapter:
This chapter trains the writer to distrust easy equivalence. The operative question is not “can the reader roughly guess the meaning?” but “did the word land in its proper slot?”

Representative stable snippets from the OCR:
- "ومن ذلك ... الحشمة ..." — used to challenge the casual reduction of the word to simple embarrassment.
- "ومن ذلك ... القافلة ..." — used to narrow the term toward the returning group.
- "ومن ذلك ... الظل والفئ ..." — used to mark an important descriptive distinction.

CHAPTER 2 — باب تأويل ما جاء مثنّى في مستعمل الكلام
Approx. pages: 38–43
Working value: conventional dual forms and paired shorthand that carry compressed cultural meaning.

Core editorial reading:
This chapter explains why Arabic often names two things together under one compact dual phrase. These expressions are not random ornaments; they are dense cultural shortcuts. A writer who knows them can read older texts accurately and avoid clumsy paraphrase.

Selected examples and takeaways:
- الأطيبان: food and sex.
- الأحمران: in one usage, meat and wine.
- الأصغران: gold and saffron; elsewhere heart and tongue appear in similar compressed pairings.
- الأبيضان / الأسودان: compressed pair-names used by context, not by literal color only.
- العمران: may denote paired proper names or paired times depending on context.
- الأبوان: often extends beyond the strict biological father/mother pairing in inherited Arabic usage.

Writing value:
- Do not decode dual compounds literally every time.
- Ask what cultural pair the expression conventionally indexes.
- When paraphrasing for modern readers, unpack the pair instead of preserving ambiguity.

CHAPTER 3 — باب تأويل المستعمل من مزدوج الكلام
Approx. pages: 44–48
Working value: binomials, fixed doubles, and rhetorical pairings.

Core editorial reading:
Ibn Qutaybah records stock doubled expressions whose two halves are not freely replaceable. The force of the phrase lies in inherited pairing, not in ad hoc synonym stacking.

Examples highlighted in the batch:
- الليّ والرمّ
- الحرّ والريح
- دبّ ودرج
- لا يعرف هِرًّا من بِرٍّ
- حيّاك الله وبيّاك / related greeting formulas discussed around this section
- حادف وقاذف / lexical pairing with role distinction
- ثاغية وراغية

Writing value:
- Treat inherited doubles as structured units.
- When revising Arabic prose, check whether a familiar pair has an established order and sense.
- Avoid fake eloquence by inventing arbitrary binaries that sound classical but have no rooted usage.

CHAPTER 4 — باب ما يستعمل من الدعاء في الكلام
Approx. pages: 48–49
Working value: supplicatory formulas, socially embedded phrasing, and what counts as idiomatic prayer-language.

Core editorial reading:
This short section shows that even prayer-like expressions have idiomatic boundaries. The writer must know what is actually said, what each formula wishes for, and when the phrase is inherited rather than analytically transparent.

Usable takeaways:
- Formulaic blessing language should be learned as usage, not assembled mechanically word by word.
- Some expressions invoke settlement, nearness, relief, companionship, or alignment rather than generic “good wishes.”
- The source’s concern is not piety alone; it is phraseological correctness.

CHAPTER 5 — باب تأويل كلام من كلام الناس مستعمل
Approx. pages: 49–64
Working value: idiom origins, semantic archaeology, stronger editorial judgment about clichés and inherited sayings.

Core editorial reading:
This chapter is especially valuable for agents and modern writers because it explains the roots of common sayings. Ibn Qutaybah does not merely gloss them; he shows the concrete image or material origin behind the expression. That origin restores force to phrases that might otherwise be repeated mechanically.

Selected expressions and practical readings:

1) "جرى عليه الدهر أشطره"
- Root image taken from the divided structure of the camel’s teats / quarters.
- Writing value: classical idiom often comes from physical partition or pastoral life.

2) "دفع إليه برمّته"
- Explained through giving something over with its tether/whole attachment.
- Writing value: use when total transfer is intended, not partial concession.

3) "ما به قلبة" / similar disease-origin expressions
- Bodily or animal disease imagery becomes a general formula for defect or hidden affliction.
- Writing value: many idioms arise from technical bodily vocabulary; flattening them loses force.

4) "نسيج وحده"
- Derived from the uniquely woven cloth with no shared loom-pattern.
- Writing value: one of the clearest source-grounded paths from craft image to praise of singular excellence.

5) "على يدي عدل"
- Tied to a named historical/policing figure and then generalized into hopeless disposal or decisive finishing.
- Writing value: some phrases keep a proper-name fossil inside them.

6) "رفع عقيرته"
- Backed by a bodily image, then generalized to raising the voice in shouting or crying.
- Writing value: recover image before reuse.

7) "لا تُبع عليه"
- Bodily/animal origin behind what later sounds like abstract discouragement.
- Writing value: many admonitory phrases are concrete before they are moral.

8) "تدين تدان"
- Presented as reciprocal consequence: as you do, so shall be done to you.
- Writing value: compact moral reciprocity formula.

9) "لا جرم"
- The chapter records grammatical-semantic discussion showing how a phrase becomes fixed usage.
- Writing value: some expressions should be treated as settled discourse particles, not re-literalized each time.

10) naming transfers such as الوضوء / الاستنجاء / التغوط / الكنيف
- The source traces how place, act, instrument, or surrounding condition can give a word its later technical sense.
- Writing value: semantic transfer is systematic; precise prose benefits from knowing the direction of transfer.

11) ظعينة / راوية / عقال / إسار
- The chapter repeatedly shows naming by relation: carrier, binding, loading, enclosure, accompaniment.
- Writing value: classical Arabic often names by function and attached context.

Editorial summary of the chapter:
A strong writer should not repeat idioms as dead furniture. This chapter revives them by explaining their original scene, material basis, or analogical extension.
